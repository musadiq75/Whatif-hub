## SCENE 3 — THE WORLD OUTSIDE — starts ≈2:40 · ≈300 words ≈ 2m14s

Outside, everyone moves the same way: leaning forward, taking short steps, like hikers carrying loaded packs. Except every hiker is carrying themselves. <break short>
Birds are the first surprise. A sparrow now weighs double — but here's the twist almost everyone gets wrong. When gravity doubles, the atmosphere itself is pulled down and compressed. The air at the surface becomes measurably denser — in the simplest physics model, nearly twice as dense. Denser air means more lift for every wingbeat. <break short>
Twice the weight. Nearly twice the lift. For a bird already on the wing, the two cancel almost exactly. What changes is everything around the flying: takeoffs turn labored, landings arrive heavy, and the survivors fly low and careful. This is informed reasoning, not a certainty. But the sky would look different by lunchtime. <break short>
The same near-cancellation happens for the jetliners already airborne at midnight. A cruising aircraft keeps flying — twice the weight, twice the air, almost a wash. But the drag on its body roughly doubles too, so every mile burns close to twice the fuel. Flights start diverting within the hour, and every landing now arrives with double the weight on every wheel and double the energy for the brakes to eat. It's a bad night for runways. Some of them are closed for good by morning. <break short>
Cars barely notice. Your engine moves the car, not you. Bridges are different. Engineers design them with safety factors — margin above expected loads. Doubling live weight doesn't snap the modern ones. It eats the margin. The failures come one at a time, for weeks, in the old bridges, the corroded ones, the ones built cheap. That's the pattern to remember: nothing breaks at midnight. Everything starts breaking in slow motion.
